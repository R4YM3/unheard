## What is emotional abuse?

Emotional abuse is a repeated pattern in which someone belittles, frightens or controls another person, or cuts them off from other people. It usually happens without physical violence and leaves no visible marks. That is exactly why it is hard to name, even for the person going through it.

The word "abuse" can feel heavy. So it helps to look at two things: **what keeps coming back**, and **what it does to you**. One harsh remark in an argument hurts, but it is not yet a pattern. This is about behaviour that repeats, and that makes your self-confidence, freedom or safety shrink further and further.

You will also come across other words, such as psychological violence or psychological abuse. In its Prevalence Monitor on Domestic Violence, CBS (Statistics Netherlands) uses the term **psychological violence in the domestic sphere** (in Dutch: _psychisch geweld in huiselijke kring_). This covers structural verbal aggression, at least monthly, and forms of coercive control in which threats and intimidation play a part. It concerns someone from the domestic sphere, and that is wider than just a partner or ex.

Emotional abuse is not a diagnosis and says nothing about the other person's personality. It describes behaviour and its effect.

## What can it look like?

Emotional abuse does not have one single form. Often it is a mix of behaviours that seem small on their own, but together form a pattern. A few examples, made up for illustration:

- **Belittling and mocking.** Constant comments about how you talk, look or do things, sometimes passed off "as a joke", even when other people are around.
- **Undermining your reality.** Being told again and again that something did not happen, that you remember it wrongly or are exaggerating, until you start to doubt your own memory. This is also called gaslighting.
- **Turning the blame around.** You bring something up, and by the end of the conversation you are the one saying sorry. DARVO is a specific form of this: denying, attacking, and reversing the roles of victim and offender. The page on [DARVO](nl/begrippen/darvo/) (in Dutch) covers this.
- **Punishing with silence or rejection.** Being ignored for days after a disagreement, without it being clear what is wrong or how to make it right again.
- **Switching between warmth and coldness.** Periods of a lot of attention and affection, alternating with coldness or anger. This makes it hard to step back, because you keep hoping for the good moments.

### Structural patterns: power, dependence and isolation

Beyond separate behaviours, a wider structure can develop. **Coercive control** is a pattern in which someone decides more and more about another person's life: who you see, where you are, what you spend, what you are allowed to think. Not every rule is control. But if your space gets smaller step by step, and saying no costs you more and more, that deserves attention.

**Power and dependence** often play a part in this. Dependence can be financial, but also practical or emotional: a shared home, children, a residence permit, a shared circle of friends. The more dependent you are, the harder it becomes to set boundaries. Money and debt are covered in a separate topic, [financial abuse](nl/begrijp-het/financieel-misbruik/) (in Dutch).

**Isolation** often happens gradually. Friends become less welcome, family visits bring more and more tension, and in the end it seems easier to stop going. **Social pressure** can also play a part, for example when the people around you get a one-sided picture of what happens at home, or when others are used to put pressure on you. These terms are explained separately in the [glossary](nl/begrippen/) (in Dutch).

## When can you not simply draw a conclusion?

Recognising something is valuable, but it is not a conclusion. A few things to keep in mind:

- **Every relationship has conflict.** People say things in arguments that they later regret. That is not the same as a pattern of control. The difference is explained in [Unhealthy conflict or a pattern?](nl/begrijp-het/conflict-of-patroon/) (in Dutch).
- **One example proves nothing.** Someone may genuinely remember a conversation differently. Only when something keeps coming back and structurally makes your space smaller does it become a pattern.
- **Both partners can behave badly.** Sometimes there is no clear split in who does what. Then it can help to look mainly at who keeps adapting, and who is afraid.
- **You cannot judge intentions from a distance.** Behaviour can be harmful even if someone does not mean it that way. And a website cannot judge what is going on in your relationship.

### Men experience it too

Emotional abuse can happen to anyone, whatever their gender. Even so, men sometimes take longer to recognise it. The image of a "victim" does not fit what many men have learned about themselves: be strong, don't complain, sort it out yourself. On top of that, emotional abuse leaves no bruises. Then it is tempting to think it is not that bad, or that you brought it on yourself. The fear of not being believed, or of being laughed at, also keeps many men quiet. That stigma makes it all the more important to take your own experience seriously.

## What it can do to you

Emotional abuse often affects how you think about yourself. People describe, for example, that they [kept doubting themselves](en/what-do-i-feel/i-doubt-myself/), felt guilty about everything, were constantly on their guard, or no longer recognised themselves. Not everyone experiences this, and it can also have other causes. There is more about this in [What it can do to you](nl/begrijp-het/wat-het-met-je-kan-doen/) (in Dutch).

## What can help?

There is no fixed route, but a few things can help you get a grip again:

- **Taking your own perception seriously.** You do not need to be sure it is abuse before you are allowed to say that something hurts you.
- **Sharing it with someone.** A friend, family member or someone you trust can look at it with you and help you sort things out. Choose someone you feel safe with.
- **Asking for advice.** Your huisarts (GP, your family doctor) can help you think through what you need. The Dutch website Thuisarts also describes what you can do if your partner or ex abuses you. At Veilig Thuis (the Dutch advice and reporting centre for domestic violence and child abuse) you can ask for advice free of charge and anonymously on [0800-2000](tel:08002000), even if you are not sure your situation is "bad enough".
- **Not confronting if that could be unsafe.** Ask for confidential advice first, before you take big steps.

If you are thinking about suicide, call [113](tel:113) or chat via 113.nl.
