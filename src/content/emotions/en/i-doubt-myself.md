## Do you recognise this?

### You keep having the conversation again. But in your head.

You search for the one sentence that would have let you say it differently. You ask someone else whether your reaction was normal. Or in the end you just say nothing, because you are afraid of being misunderstood again.

> Maybe it’s me. Why do I keep thinking that?

Self-doubt is sometimes part of life and of relationships. But if you notice that you trust less and less what you notice, feel or need, that deserves attention.

It can start small. You read a message five times before you send it. You begin sentences with “maybe I’m seeing it wrong, but…”. After a conversation you no longer know exactly what it was about, only that you seemed to be wrong again. Your memory feels clear at first, and later it doesn’t.

Maybe you also learned that you shouldn’t make a fuss, that you have to be strong or sort it out yourself. Many men recognise that. Then doubt quickly feels like weakness, and you talk about it even less. But doubt is not a character flaw. It is a signal that something is weighing on you.

## What happens around you

Self-doubt rarely comes out of nowhere. Often a conversation, or a series of conversations, comes first. Maybe you recognise one or more of these situations:

- You describe something you went through, and are told it didn’t happen that way.
- Your feelings are called “over the top”, “too sensitive” or “drama”.
- A conversation about your worry ends with something you supposedly did wrong.
- You ask for an explanation, and get a question back about why you are so suspicious.
- Other people are brought in: “everyone thinks you…”.

Any of these things can happen in an ordinary argument or on a bad day. People remember things differently, and nobody always responds with care. What can make the difference is repetition: does it happen once, or do you notice that your experience counts for less, again and again?

## What might explain it

Sometimes it is a misunderstanding or a difficult conversation. Sometimes you recognise a recurring pattern in which there is less and less room for your experience.

If someone repeatedly denies or twists what happened, or undermines what you noticed, that can affect your trust in yourself. This is sometimes called gaslighting. One difference in memory is not enough to draw that conclusion, and the word says nothing about what someone intends or who they are.

Another pattern is a conversation that quietly turns around: you start with your pain and end up apologising. There is a term for this, [DARVO](nl/begrippen/darvo/) (in Dutch), which describes how denying, attacking and reversing the roles can follow one another.

Self-doubt can also have other roots. Earlier experiences can make you quicker to look for the fault in yourself. Stress, little sleep or a hard period make it more difficult to think clearly. Often several things play a part at once. You don’t have to choose which explanation is right to notice what it does to you.

## What it can do to you

In the short term, doubt mostly costs energy. You worry, sleep worse or keep replaying a conversation. You put off decisions, because you are not sure whether your own judgement is right.

In the longer term, doubt can settle into how you see yourself. You start asking whether you are allowed to feel something before you feel it. You leave choices to others. You keep looking for reassurance, and it only brings calm for a short while. That last point is the subject of the page [Ik zoek steeds bevestiging](nl/wat-voel-ik/ik-zoek-steeds-bevestiging/) (“I keep looking for reassurance”, in Dutch).

The good news is cautious, but real: trust in what you notice can come back, step by step. On [Zelfvertrouwen terugvinden](nl/herstel/zelfvertrouwen/) (“Finding your self-confidence again”, in Dutch) you can read what that can look like.

## When is it something else?

Not every doubt points to a pattern. Wondering whether you handled something well, whether you hurt someone, or whether your memory is right: that is part of being an honest person. Healthy self-reflection usually feels like curiosity. Self-doubt that chafes feels more like losing your footing.

The doubt may also come mainly from yourself, separate from anyone else. For example, because you are going through a period in which everything feels heavy. If doubt comes together with lasting low mood, anxiety, sleeping badly or the feeling that you are no longer coping well, your GP (huisarts) is a good first step. They can look with you at what help fits.

## A next step

Separate what was said, what you felt and what you need now. Find someone to talk to who listens without deciding for you what you should think or do. Only write something down if that feels safe. The [self-reflection card](nl/hulpmiddelen/wat-voel-ik-en-wat-heb-ik-nodig/) (in Dutch) can help with this.
